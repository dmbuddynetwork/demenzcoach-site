You hear:

“I have to call someone right now!” – when someone with dementia wants the phone

What's behind it

Wanting to call often means: I want to reach someone, I'm worried, or something needs sorting.

What you can say

“You want to talk to someone. Who shall we call?”

“Let's call together. I'll dial.”

“Tell me what's on your mind first.”

Are they crying or very upset? Then just say: “I'm with you.” The sentences above help once things are a little calmer.

These are suggestions, not rules. Adjust the words and watch how they land. Add your names in the translator

What you can do

Save important numbers with photos on a simple phone.

Didn't help? Go to the second try ↓

Said in other ways: “Give me the phone!”, “I want to call my son.”

If that doesn't help

“Let's call together after coffee. I'll remind you.”

Respond to the worry: sit down, ask what's on their mind, and offer to call together later.

It's okay to stop. If it gets louder or more heated: stop talking, stop asking for anything, step back if it's safe, and start again later. If anyone is in danger: call 911 (US) or 999 (UK).

More about this

Background

You can take that seriously and help them get in touch, or respond to the worry.

Every moment is different. Often it's one of these:

  • Worry about someone.
  • Wanting closeness and contact.
  • Feeling that something needs to be sorted out.
  • Restlessness.

Better to avoid

Things that often don't help:

  • saying “You just called”
  • taking the phone away without a word
  • getting annoyed

What can help next

  • Agree fixed call times with the family.
  • Watch out for scam calls. Banks never ask for a PIN on the phone.

When talking isn't enough

In danger, after a fall, with breathing problems or unconsciousness, or if you're unsure: call 911 (US) or 999 (UK). For medical advice, contact your doctor. This tool can't judge how urgent something is.
