“Help! Help!” – when someone with dementia keeps calling out

What's behind it

Calling out is often a sign that something doesn't feel right: loneliness, fear, boredom or a need they can't name.

What you can say

“I'm here. I heard you.”

“You called. What do you need?”

“I'll sit with you for a while.”

Are they crying or very upset? Then just say: “I'm with you.” The sentences above help once things are a little calmer.

These are suggestions, not rules. Adjust the words and watch how they land. Add your names in the translator

What you can do

Go and look: toilet, a drink, how they're sitting or lying, warmth, light.

Didn't help? Go to the second try ↓

Said in other ways: “Hello? Is anybody there?”, “Nurse! Nurse!”

If that doesn't help

“I'm staying right here with you.”

Stay close for a moment without speaking: sit down, hold a hand if it feels right, play a familiar song.

It's okay to stop. If it gets louder or more heated: stop talking, stop asking for anything, step back if it's safe, and start again later. If anyone is in danger: call 911 (US) or 999 (UK).

More about this

Background

Instead of only saying “shh”, you can go and look and offer closeness.

Every moment is different. Often it's one of these:

  • Feeling alone or scared.
  • A need they can't put into words, like the toilet, a drink, or being too hot or too cold.
  • Boredom or too little going on.
  • Not knowing where they are.

Better to avoid

Things that often don't help:

  • ignoring it or just saying “Be quiet”
  • telling them off
  • leaving them alone for long

What can help next

  • Some people like soft music or something to hold, like a cushion or a soft cloth.
  • If you're unsure what's behind it, talk to your doctor.

When talking isn't enough

In danger, after a fall, with breathing problems or unconsciousness, or if you're unsure: call 911 (US) or 999 (UK). For medical advice, contact your doctor. This tool can't judge how urgent something is.
