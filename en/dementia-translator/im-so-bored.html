You hear:

“I'm so bored” – when someone with dementia has nothing to do

What's behind it

Boredom often means: I want to be part of things and do something that matters.

What you can say

“Could you help me with this? I'd be glad.”

“Shall we sort the laundry together?”

“Let's go for a little walk.”

Are they crying or very upset? Then just say: “I'm with you.” The sentences above help once things are a little calmer.

These are suggestions, not rules. Adjust the words and watch how they land. Add your names in the translator

What you can do

Simple tasks: folding towels, sorting buttons, peeling vegetables, watering plants.

Didn't help? Go to the second try ↓

Said in other ways: “I'm just sitting around.”, “What am I supposed to do?”

If that doesn't help

“Here, hold this for me. You're a great help.”

Instead of asking “What would you like to do?”, just start something and invite them in: sorting, folding, stirring.

It's okay to stop. If it gets louder or more heated: stop talking, stop asking for anything, step back if it's safe, and start again later. If anyone is in danger: call 911 (US) or 999 (UK).

More about this

Background

Instead of entertainment, simple everyday tasks you do together are often enough.

Every moment is different. Often it's one of these:

  • Wanting to be useful.
  • Too little going on, or tasks that are too hard.
  • Feeling lonely.
  • Restlessness.

Better to avoid

Things that often don't help:

  • saying “Just rest”
  • giving tasks that are too hard
  • switching on the TV and leaving

What can help next

  • Music from their younger years, or looking at photos together.
  • A daily routine with a few fixed points.

When talking isn't enough

In danger, after a fall, with breathing problems or unconsciousness, or if you're unsure: call 911 (US) or 999 (UK). For medical advice, contact your doctor. This tool can't judge how urgent something is.
