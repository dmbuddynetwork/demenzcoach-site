“I'm not changing my clothes” – getting dressed with someone with dementia

What's behind it

Familiar clothes give a sense of safety. Too many choices or rushing can feel overwhelming.

What you can say

“You look good in that. Would you like the blue one or the red one?”

“Let's freshen up a little.”

“Take your time. I'll hand you everything.”

Are they crying or very upset? Then just say: “I'm with you.” The sentences above help once things are a little calmer.

These are suggestions, not rules. Adjust the words and watch how they land. Add your names in the translator

What you can do

Lay out clothes in the order they go on.

Didn't help? Go to the second try ↓

Said in other ways: “These clothes are still clean.”, “These aren't my clothes.”

If that doesn't help

“Let's just change your top. The rest we'll do later.”

Leave it for now. Try again later, maybe after a wash or before going out, with just one item.

It's okay to stop. If it gets louder or more heated: stop talking, stop asking for anything, step back if it's safe, and start again later. If anyone is in danger: call 911 (US) or 999 (UK).

More about this

Background

Instead of arguing, you can offer fewer choices and more time.

Every moment is different. Often it's one of these:

  • Familiar clothes feel safe.
  • Too many steps or choices at once.
  • Feeling embarrassed about undressing.
  • Not recognizing the clothes.

Better to avoid

Things that often don't help:

  • saying “That's dirty”
  • laying out too many choices
  • rushing

What can help next

  • Buy a favorite item twice, so one can be washed.
  • Offer two choices, not a full closet.

When talking isn't enough

In danger, after a fall, with breathing problems or unconsciousness, or if you're unsure: call 911 (US) or 999 (UK). For medical advice, contact your doctor. This tool can't judge how urgent something is.
