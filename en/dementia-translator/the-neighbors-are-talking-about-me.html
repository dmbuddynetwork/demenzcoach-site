“The neighbors are talking about me” – when someone with dementia feels watched

What's behind it

Feeling watched or talked about is unsettling. Gaps in memory and misheard words can turn into a story that feels real.

What you can say

“That sounds unpleasant. I'm on your side.”

“You want to feel safe at home. I understand.”

“Let's sit together. I'm staying here.”

Are they crying or very upset? Then just say: “I'm with you.” The sentences above help once things are a little calmer.

These are suggestions, not rules. Adjust the words and watch how they land. Add your names in the translator

What you can do

Have familiar people around more often, so home feels safe.

Didn't help? Go to the second try ↓

Said in other ways: “The neighbors come into my apartment.”, “Everyone is whispering about me.”

If that doesn't help

“Let's put some music on and have a cup of tea.”

Don't discuss the neighbors any further. Gently change the scene: a drink, some music, a short walk together.

It's okay to stop. If it gets louder or more heated: stop talking, stop asking for anything, step back if it's safe, and start again later. If anyone is in danger: call 911 (US) or 999 (UK).

More about this

Background

You can take the feeling seriously without confirming the story.

Every moment is different. Often it's one of these:

  • Feeling unsure and afraid.
  • Misheard or misunderstood words.
  • Gaps in memory filled with an explanation.
  • Feeling lonely or left out.

Better to avoid

Things that often don't help:

  • agreeing with the story
  • insisting “That's nonsense”
  • confronting the neighbors

What can help next

  • Check together that doors and windows are closed.
  • If you're unsure what's going on, talk to your doctor.

When talking isn't enough

In danger, after a fall, with breathing problems or unconsciousness, or if you're unsure: call 911 (US) or 999 (UK). For medical advice, contact your doctor. This tool can't judge how urgent something is.
