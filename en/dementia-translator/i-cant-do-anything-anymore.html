“I can't do anything anymore” – when someone with dementia feels useless

What's behind it

Behind this is often grief about what they have lost, and the need to still matter.

What you can say

“That's hard when things don't work like they used to.”

“You matter to me. Just as you are.”

“Could you help me with this? You're good at it.”

Are they crying or very upset? Then just say: “I'm with you.” The sentences above help once things are a little calmer.

These are suggestions, not rules. Adjust the words and watch how they land. Add your names in the translator

What you can do

Give small tasks they can still do, like sorting, folding or stirring.

Didn't help? Go to the second try ↓

Said in other ways: “I'm so stupid.”, “I'm just a burden.”

If that doesn't help

“Come on, let's do it together. You fold, I'll stack.”

Don't argue with the feeling. Sit with them and do something small together that goes well, like folding towels.

It's okay to stop. If it gets louder or more heated: stop talking, stop asking for anything, step back if it's safe, and start again later. If anyone is in danger: call 911 (US) or 999 (UK).

More about this

Background

Instead of quick reassurance, you can acknowledge the feeling and offer small moments of success.

Every moment is different. Often it's one of these:

  • Grief about abilities that are fading.
  • Shame after a mistake.
  • Wanting to still be needed.
  • Feeling lonely or bored.

Better to avoid

Things that often don't help:

  • brushing it off with “That's not true”
  • doing everything for them
  • correcting them in front of others

What can help next

  • Praise the effort, not just the result.
  • If you're worried about how they are feeling, talk to your doctor.

When talking isn't enough

In danger, after a fall, with breathing problems or unconsciousness, or if you're unsure: call 911 (US) or 999 (UK). For medical advice, contact your doctor. This tool can't judge how urgent something is.
