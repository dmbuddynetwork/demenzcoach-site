“Don't leave me!” – when someone with dementia follows you everywhere

What's behind it

Behind this is often fear: you are their anchor in a confusing world. Instead of slipping out, you can say goodbye clearly and leave something familiar behind.

What you can say

“I'm just going to the kitchen. I'll be right back.”

“I'll be back soon. Here's your tea.”

“I'm here. Hold my hand.”

Are they crying or very upset? Then just say: “I'm with you.” The sentences above help once things are a little calmer.

These are suggestions, not rules. Adjust the words and watch how they land. Add your names in the translator

What you can do

Leave a note: “I'm in the kitchen. Back soon.”

Didn't help? Go to the second try ↓

Said in other ways: “Stay with me!”, “Where are you going?”

If that doesn't help

“I'll be back after this song. Listen, it's your favorite.”

Give them something to hold on to: a task, a blanket, a song. Then tell them clearly when you'll be back.

It's okay to stop. If it gets louder or more heated: stop talking, stop asking for anything, step back if it's safe, and start again later. If anyone is in danger: call 911 (US) or 999 (UK).

More about this

Other possible reasons

Every moment is different. Often it's one of these:

  • Fear of being alone.
  • You feel like the only safe point.
  • Not knowing when you'll be back.
  • Loneliness.

Better to avoid

Things that often don't help:

  • slipping out secretly
  • getting irritated
  • long explanations of why you have to go

What can help next

  • A familiar person, music or a simple task while you're away.
  • You need breaks too. Ask about respite care or a sitting service.

When talking isn't enough

In danger, after a fall, with breathing problems or unconsciousness, or if you're unsure: call 911 (US) or 999 (UK). For medical advice, contact your doctor. This tool can't judge how urgent something is.
