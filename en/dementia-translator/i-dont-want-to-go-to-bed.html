You hear:

“I don't want to go to bed” – bedtime with someone with dementia

What's behind it

Bedtime can feel unsettling: darkness, being alone, or not knowing what comes next.

What you can say

“You can stay up a little longer. I'll keep you company.”

“Let's get comfortable first. Then we'll see.”

“I'll come with you and stay for a bit.”

Are they crying or very upset? Then just say: “I'm with you.” The sentences above help once things are a little calmer.

These are suggestions, not rules. Adjust the words and watch how they land. Add your names in the translator

What you can do

A fixed evening routine with the same steps each night.

Didn't help? Go to the second try ↓

Said in other ways: “I'm not tired.”, “I'm not going to sleep yet.”

If that doesn't help

“Let's sit here for a bit. We'll go to bed later.”

Drop the bed for now: sit together on the sofa with a blanket and try again later.

It's okay to stop. If it gets louder or more heated: stop talking, stop asking for anything, step back if it's safe, and start again later. If anyone is in danger: call 911 (US) or 999 (UK).

More about this

Background

Instead of insisting on a time, you can make the evening familiar and unhurried.

Every moment is different. Often it's one of these:

  • Not feeling tired.
  • Fear of the dark or of being alone.
  • Not being sure what time it is.
  • Wanting to decide for themselves.

Better to avoid

Things that often don't help:

  • insisting “It's bedtime now”
  • rushing
  • bright light and a loud TV right before bed

What can help next

  • Dim lights and quiet music in the evening.
  • You need sleep too. Ask about respite care or overnight help.

When talking isn't enough

In danger, after a fall, with breathing problems or unconsciousness, or if you're unsure: call 911 (US) or 999 (UK). For medical advice, contact your doctor. This tool can't judge how urgent something is.
