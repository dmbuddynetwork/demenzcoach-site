“When are we leaving?” – when someone with dementia asks the same question again and again

What's behind it

The question often comes from feeling unsure: the answer is gone again a few minutes later.

What you can say

“We'll go after lunch. I'll let you know.”

“There's plenty of time. I'm here.”

“Let's have a coffee first. I'll tell you when it's time.”

Are they crying or very upset? Then just say: “I'm with you.” The sentences above help once things are a little calmer.

These are suggestions, not rules. Adjust the words and watch how they land. Add your names in the translator

What you can do

Write the plan on a large note or a whiteboard where they can see it.

Didn't help? Go to the second try ↓

Said in other ways: “What time is it?”, “What day is it today?”

If that doesn't help

“Everything's sorted. Let's have a cup of tea first.”

Answer the feeling rather than the question: sit together, have a drink and gently change the subject.

It's okay to stop. If it gets louder or more heated: stop talking, stop asking for anything, step back if it's safe, and start again later. If anyone is in danger: call 911 (US) or 999 (UK).

More about this

Background

Instead of saying “I've told you already”, you can answer briefly and kindly, and respond to the worry behind it.

Every moment is different. Often it's one of these:

  • The answer they just got is no longer there.
  • Worry about missing something.
  • Not being sure how the day is going.
  • Wanting closeness and contact.

Better to avoid

Things that often don't help:

  • saying “I've already told you that”
  • long explanations
  • talking about plans too far ahead

What can help next

  • Mention appointments as late as you reasonably can.
  • Take a break for yourself when the repeating wears you down.

When talking isn't enough

In danger, after a fall, with breathing problems or unconsciousness, or if you're unsure: call 911 (US) or 999 (UK). For medical advice, contact your doctor. This tool can't judge how urgent something is.
