“Where am I?” – when someone with dementia doesn't know where they are

What's behind it

Not knowing where you are is frightening. First give a sense of safety, then a short, simple answer.

What you can say

“You're safe. I'm here with you.”

“We're at home, in the living room. Everything is all right.”

“Come and sit with me for a moment.”

Are they crying or very upset? Then just say: “I'm with you.” The sentences above help once things are a little calmer.

These are suggestions, not rules. Adjust the words and watch how they land. Add your names in the translator

What you can do

Keep familiar things in sight: photos, a blanket, their chair.

Didn't help? Go to the second try ↓

Said in other ways: “What am I doing here?”, “I don't know where I am.”

If that doesn't help

“I'm right here. You're not alone.”

Use fewer words: sit next to them, hold a hand if it feels right, and stay quiet for a moment.

It's okay to stop. If it gets louder or more heated: stop talking, stop asking for anything, step back if it's safe, and start again later. If anyone is in danger: call 911 (US) or 999 (UK).

More about this

Background

Long explanations are often too much in that moment.

Every moment is different. Often it's one of these:

  • The place feels unfamiliar right now.
  • Fear and feeling unsure.
  • Memories of an earlier home.
  • Tiredness or too much going on.

Better to avoid

Things that often don't help:

  • quizzing them (“Don't you know where you are?”)
  • long explanations
  • leaving them alone

What can help next

  • Good light, less noise, fewer people at once.
  • If you're unsure what's going on, talk to your doctor.

When talking isn't enough

In danger, after a fall, with breathing problems or unconsciousness, or if you're unsure: call 911 (US) or 999 (UK). For medical advice, contact your doctor. This tool can't judge how urgent something is.
